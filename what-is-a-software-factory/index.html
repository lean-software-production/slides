Software Factory:
a working definition

A software factory combines multiple agents working together, with some kind of feedback or validation loop, allowing them to work autonomously towards a desired outcome.

References: strongDM · Harness engineering for coding agent users

MATT owns this slide. This is his half of the double act. Ninety seconds.

This is the shoulders-of-giants beat, so make it generous. People have got a long way already, and they got there in about three years. Say what they achieved before you say anything else.

Plain language, the words you would use to an engineer you just met in a lift. No jargon they have to accept on trust. Lead with multiple agents working towards an outcome, and the feedback or validation loop that checks their work.

Then point at the picture. Something makes the thing, something else checks it, and if the check fails it goes round again. That loop is why it is a factory and not you typing into a REPL.

Credit the sources by name. Do not sneer. Matt's position on the record: this is how the industry uses the word, and we are not going to change that by insisting.

Software Factory:
a precise definition

Out in the industry

"Software Factory" is a broad term for automating repetitive tasks using LLMs and feedback loops. You might also say "harness" or "user harness".

Useful, but imprecise.

Inside the factory

Once you're actually working on a factory, building and improving it, you realize it's made up of pieces that need their own names.

Towards a ubiquitous language.

What's inside the factory?

  • Machine model + harness
  • Doer & validator two useful machine archetypes
  • Assembly line machines wired together for a particular purpose
  • Orchestrator runs the assembly lines and watches over them
  • Visibility & controls the buttons and dials that give you visibility and control over your factory

Machine

Handed its inputs, runs to completion, returns a result.

ALDRIC. Ninety seconds.

This is where harness gets pinned down, as promised on the bridge slide. Model plus harness equals agent. An agent given one job to run unattended is a machine. Name the tools out loud, because the definition lands the moment people can put products in it.

Do not say "no human in the conversation". That is true of a dark factory and not of the factories we actually want. The thing that makes it a machine is that it runs unattended for the duration of its job, not that humans are barred.

The bash script point lands well, and it is why we say machine and not agent. Not everything in a factory is AI, and people relax when they hear that.

Doer & validator

The validator checks for mistakes and gives feedback.

Using a separate agent with fresh context avoids bias. Using a different model gives even better results.

MATT has this one. Ninety seconds. This is the idea most worth protecting if we run long.

Open on the shared pain: the model says it is done, and it is not done. Everybody coding with LLMs has had that morning. Sometimes it is awful, sometimes it is nearly right, and either way you have to check.

Then the Tetris story. On the course we hand students a loop that builds a Tetris game and let it run unattended. Everybody's Tetris comes out completely different, because there is no validator holding it to anything.

Then the turn: "The interesting engineering is not the doing. It is deciding what done means precisely enough that something other than you can check it."

Do not claim a loop without a validator is not a factory. We argued about that and did not settle it. Say it is a bad one, and move on.

Hand back to ALDRIC: one trustworthy step is not a factory yet, it is one step.

Assembly line

Wires multiple machines together in a meaningful way: takes a seed as input, works on it, produces some artefact.

This could be spec → working code, but it could also be flickering test → fix PR.

ALDRIC. Ninety seconds.

Emphasise that the shape is decided by a human, up front. This is the sentence that separates a factory from a big prompt and a prayer. Design lives in the topology, not in the model's head at runtime.

Aldric's own note: the abstract version is dry, so land it with something they have already built. If you have used Dagster, or Airflow, or Hadoop, or done any data pipeline work at all, you have built one of these before. That lands harder than the definition does.

The second line lights up here too. One or more, which is exactly where we part company with the looser definition earlier.

Ten seconds on seed: whatever you feed in. A vague idea, a screenshot, a bug report, a full spec. People assume you need a spec. You do not.

Hand to MATT: the line just sits there until something runs it.

Orchestrator

The line is inert on its own. You point the orchestrator at something, "go look at issue 341", and it starts a line, hands each machine its inputs, and decides what happens when something goes wrong.

Was that a flaky failure or a real one? Retry, start over, or throw the work away? How many machines run at once, how long before we kill one, how many times round the loop. The line is dumb. The orchestrator makes those calls.

MATT. Ninety seconds. Aldric can interrupt if it wobbles, and that is fine to say out loud.

It is the management layer. The line runs the work; the orchestrator handles the trouble, and it decides the runtime limits: parallelism, timeouts, loop counts, budget.

Aldric's framing from Stone: telling an intermittent failure from a real one is the orchestrator's job, and it is where the interesting decisions live.

The July cohort line lands here. Students built a beautiful assembly line and then ran it by hand. They were the orchestrator, so strictly nobody had a factory yet. It gets a laugh and a wince, and it shows the definition has teeth.

Note that fan-out is still just a line. Choosing how many of those run at once is orchestration. Aldric and Matt agreed that boundary on the call, so state it the same way.

Flag forward: the orchestrator is also how the factory talks to the outside world. Hand to ALDRIC.

Visibility & controls

These are the buttons and dials on your factory. You can watch it run, find out how many tokens it's burning, and even step in to intervene.

Being able to type "remember <some context>" into a running machine feels a bit like cheating, but sometimes it's very useful.

ALDRIC. This is his hill, so let him have it. Ninety seconds.

Frame it as the payoff from the orchestrator rather than a new topic. One place to see which machines are working, one place to see the spend, one place to send a message in.

Aldric's minimum, stated plainly: spend in tokens and money and CPU, per machine and per factory; the ability to open a machine and read its conversation; a summary of the work happening inside it; and the ability to steer it mid-run.

Human in the loop lives here. Matt will ask about Fabro stopping the line to message a human on Slack. That is the steer control, and Aldric answers it. The two of you already know this exchange is coming, so play it.

The software factory

A factory is one or more assembly lines of machines, an orchestrator that runs them, and the visibility and controls a human needs to stay in charge.

MATT restates it, top down, once. Sixty seconds, nothing new.

Matt's framing from the call: you can treat the factory as an abstraction, seed in, artefact out. What this model buys you is the ability to break that abstraction open and say which piece you are talking about.

Delivered from memory, no hedging. If we sound like we are working it out live, the lesson fails whatever else happens. Rehearse this sentence until it is boring.

If the chat dries up, the feelings question is the reopener. So is quoting back something from the opening chat.

Have these straight between us beforehand:

  • Is my thing a factory? Walk it up the stack out loud together. Machines? A validator that did not write the work? A line whose shape you chose? Something that runs it and handles failure? Can you see the spend and reach in? Almost nothing scores all of it yet, ours included, and saying so is more persuasive than a scorecard we would obviously win.
  • Is a Ralph loop a factory? In the loose sense, yes. In ours, not until it has an orchestrator and visibility and controls. Both of us say the same thing here.
  • Isn't this just CI? CI validates. It does not do. A factory has doers.
  • Doesn't this cost a fortune? Sometimes. It is not the hundred times we were promised, and knowing where it is not worth it is part of the skill.
  • Does it replace engineers? The judgment shifts, it does not disappear. Somebody still has to define done.
  • How do I start on Monday? One assembly line, two machines, one validator.
  • What would make you stop a run? Good one to throw back at them. Cost ceiling, a validator failing the same way three times, CPU pinned for fifteen minutes with no output.

Win condition: people leave thinking these two know what they are talking about and they agree with each other. Disagreeing on stage about the taxonomy costs us more than any missed content.

Parting thoughts:

Does a Software Factory have to be a Dark Factory?

ALDRIC leads, MATT brings the centaurs. Two minutes, and this is the last thing they hear. This is our spicy take and we are allowed to be a bit political.

The misconception worth naming: people think this is the thing that comes for their job, and that it is stupid because they are useful. They are right. It does not take the job. It is a power tool in your hand.

ALDRIC has the argument from his post. Automating the human does not just automate the typing, it erases the decisions and the context. The hard part of software is not the typing. Highly automated factories are real and strongDM built one. A factory with nobody in it is a different claim, and that one is a lie.

MATT on Doctorow: it comes down to whether you are in charge or the machine is.

Do not resolve the discomfort too neatly. If somebody said "dystopian" in the chat at the start, quote them back now. The honest answer is that the unease is appropriate, and the response to it is visibility and control rather than reassurance.
